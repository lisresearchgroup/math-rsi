Abstract
We prove that a binary linear code of length 43,
dimension 10, and minimum distance at least 17
does not exist. The MacWilliams identities force
a dual word of weight four, and shortening on
its support reduces the problem to extensions of
the 172 classiﬁed full-support [40, 7, 18] codes.
For each such code, a complete enumeration of
its 232 even cosets shows that any two distinct
cosets at distance at least 16 have a sum at dis-
tance below 16, so the required extension does
not exist. Two independent enumerations and an
independent replay of all 619,053,789 pair rejec-
tions verify this ﬁnite step. Puncturing and short-
ening then exclude [44, 10, ≥18], [44, 11, ≥17],
and [45, 11, ≥18] codes, and a reduction to the
published nonexistence of [41, 14, 14] codes ex-
cludes [42, 16, ≥13]. Together with explicit con-
structions, these results determine four optimal
minimum distances that tables of bounds have
listed as intervals since at least 1985, and give
16 ≤d2(45, 11) ≤17.
1. Introduction
For 1 ≤k ≤n, let d2(n, k) denote the largest minimum
distance of a k-dimensional subspace of Fn
2. Determining
this function is one of the basic ﬁnite problems of coding
theory. Its values for small parameters have been collected
in tables for more than ﬁve decades: Helgert & Stinaff
(1973) tabulated bounds for lengths below 128, and the
tables were updated by Verhoeff (1987) and Brouwer &
Verhoeff (1993). The online tables of Grassl (2007) con-

upper bound requires a nonexistence proof for a speciﬁc
triple (n, k, d).

Outline of the proof.
Parity extension takes a hypothet-
ical [43, 10, ≥17] code to an even [44, 10, ≥18] code C.
The exclusion of [41, 8, 18] codes (Bouyukliev et al., 2000)
forces any three generator columns of C to be independent.
A MacWilliams-identity argument then forces a weight-
four word in C⊥. Shortening on its support gives an even,
full-support [40, 7, 18] code K. Thus K belongs to one of
the 172 classiﬁed equivalence classes.
To extend K to C, one needs three distinct even cosets
ai+K, each at distance at least 16 from K, whose pairwise
sums also have distance at least 16. The even quotient has
dimension 32. We enumerate it completely for every clas-
siﬁed code and ﬁnd a stronger obstruction: no two distinct
admissible cosets have an admissible sum. The extension
is therefore impossible before the condition on the sum of
all three cosets is even needed. The length-42 result instead
follows from parity extension, two shortenings, and exact-
distance normalization, using the published [41, 14, 14] ex-
clusion.

2. Related Work
Tables of bounds.
Systematic tables of bounds on
d2(n, k) go back to Helgert & Stinaff (1973). Verhoeff
(1985) reconstructed and updated that table in a technical
report, and journal updates followed in Verhoeff (1987)
and Brouwer & Verhoeff (1993). The online tables main-
tained by Grassl (2007) record the current lower and upper
bounds together with construction and derivation chains.
For the ﬁrst four parameter pairs in Table 1, the recorded
upper-bound chains end at the nonexistence of [25, 8, 10]
codes (Ytrehus & Helleseth, 1990). They propagate that
result through parity extension, shortening, and residual-
code bounds. Our results replace these chains by sharper
bounds.
The Griesmer bound and small dimensions.
For ﬁxed
k and d, the Griesmer bound (Griesmer, 1960) gives a
lower bound g2(k, d) on the length of a binary linear
[n, k, d] code. Baumert & McEliece (1973) showed that
for each ﬁxed dimension the bound is attained for all suf-
ﬁciently large d, so determining the minimum length for
ﬁxed k is a ﬁnite problem.
The minimum lengths for
k ≤7 were determined by Baumert & McEliece (1973)
and van Tilborg (1981), and the case k = 8 was completed
by Bouyukliev et al. (2000). As a result, d2(n, k) is known
for all k ≤8 (Kurz, 2021). In dimension nine, Kurz (2021)
showed that the [46, 9, 20] code is unique and excluded
[47, 10, 20] and [85, 9, 40] codes. Our main theorem con-
cerns dimension ten; its reduction rests on the classiﬁcation
for dimensions seven and eight.
Classiﬁcation of linear codes.
Exhaustive classiﬁcation
up to equivalence is the main tool for nonexistence re-
sults at moderate lengths. Kaski & Östergård (2006) give
a general treatment of classiﬁcation algorithms for codes
and designs.
Jaffe (2000) classiﬁed the optimal binary
linear codes of length at most 30.
Bouyukliev & Jaffe
(2001) classiﬁed optimal binary linear codes of dimension
at most seven; their classiﬁcation supplies the 172 full-
support [40, 7, 18] classes used here. Further classiﬁcation
and nonexistence results for codes with prescribed mini-
mum distance are given by Feulner (2014). Bouyukliev &
Jacobsson (2011) classiﬁed all codes of minimum distance
8 and codimension at most 14, and all codes of minimum
distance 10 and codimension at most 18, and excluded
[33, 18, 8] and [33, 14, 10] codes. Bouyukliev et al. (2021)
developed classiﬁcation algorithms based on canonical aug-
mentation and lattice-point enumeration. Bouyuklieva &
Bouyukliev (2020) classify codes by extending their resid-
uals; their Proposition 2 states that no [41, 14, 14] code ex-
ists, which is the input for our (42, 16) result. Bouyuk-
lieva & Dzhumalieva-Stoeva (2025) study the hulls of opti-
mal codes, and their counts provide an independent consis-

tency check on our catalogue. In our computation, coordi-
nate equivalence is tested through graph isomorphism with
nauty (McKay & Piperno, 2014).
Weight-distribution methods.
The MacWilliams identi-
ties (MacWilliams, 1963) relate the weight distributions
of a code and its dual.
Linear-programming arguments
based on them are a standard source of upper bounds
(MacWilliams & Sloane, 1977). Schrijver (2005) strength-
ened such bounds for unrestricted binary codes using
semideﬁnite programming.
We use the identities differ-
ently: a single nonnegative polynomial forces the existence
of a dual word of weight four, which converts a nonex-
istence question into an extension problem over a classi-
ﬁed subcode. Residual-code arguments combined with the
MacWilliams identities also appear in Kurz (2021).
Computer-assisted nonexistence proofs.
Large ﬁnite
searches have settled several nonexistence questions in
combinatorics; a prominent example is the nonexistence of
a projective plane of order 10 (Lam et al., 1989). Such
proofs are only as reliable as their enumeration. We there-
fore separate the completeness of each enumeration from
the validity of each rejection, cross-check completeness
with two independent implementations, and replay every
rejection with an independent checker. The deductive steps,
as distinct from these computations, are formally checked.
3. Preliminaries and Classiﬁcation Results
3.1. Notation and elementary operations
Write wt(x) for Hamming weight.
For a positive-
dimensional code C, let d(C) be its minimum nonzero
weight. Coordinate equivalence means equality after a co-
ordinate permutation. A code has full support if no coordi-
nate is identically zero, and is even if all its words have
even weight.
We use the standard operations on codes
(MacWilliams & Sloane, 1977); since several arguments
depend on their exact effect on parameters, we record the
precise statements.
Puncturing deletes coordinates. For a set S of t coordi-
nates, the kernel of puncturing consists of the codewords
supported in S. If t < d(C), this kernel is zero, so punctur-
ing is injective and preserves dimension. Every remaining
word loses at most t ones, so the punctured code has dis-
tance at least d(C) −t.
Shortening on a coordinate set S ﬁrst restricts to words zero
on S and then deletes S. Let πS : C →FS
2 be the coordi-
nate projection, of rank r. The shortened code is the image
of ker πS after deleting S, and the deletion is injective on
this kernel. By rank-nullity, shortening reduces dimension
by exactly r and changes no weight; in particular, it does
not reduce minimum distance. For one coordinate, r ≤1,
which includes the case of a coordinate that is identically
zero. When an exact smaller dimension is needed, we pass
to a subspace of that dimension.
Parity extension sends x to (x, ∑
i xi). It is injective be-
cause the ﬁrst n coordinates recover x. It preserves the
weight of even words and adds one to the weight of odd
words. Hence the extended code is even, and its minimum
distance is at least the least even integer that is at least the
original lower bound.

4. Reduction to Classiﬁed Subcodes
We prove Theorem 1.1 using E1, E2, and Proposition 5.1;
the latter is established by the ﬁnite computation of Sec-
tion 5. The reduction has three steps. We ﬁrst show that
every hypothetical code has a dual word of weight four.
Shortening on its support then produces a classiﬁed sub-
code. Finally, the hypothetical code is recovered from this
subcode by an extension whose feasibility depends only on
three cosets.
4.1. A forced dual word of weight four
Suppose that C0 is a [43, 10, ≥17] code. Its parity exten-
sion C is an even [44, 10, ≥18] code: an odd weight at
least 17 increases by one, and an even weight at least 17 is
already at least 18. Conversely, puncturing any coordinate

6. Further Nonexistence Results and Optimal
Distances
Corollary 6.1. Binary linear [44, 10, ≥18], [44, 11, ≥17],
and [45, 11, ≥18] codes do not exist.
Proof. Puncturing any coordinate of a [44, 10, ≥18] code
gives a [43, 10, ≥17] code, contradicting Theorem 1.1.
Shortening a [44, 11, ≥17] code on any coordinate gives
a length-43 code of dimension at least ten and distance at
least 17; a ten-dimensional subcode gives the same contra-
diction. Finally, puncturing a [45, 11, ≥18] code gives the
excluded [44, 11, ≥17] parameters.
E3 is stated for codes of exact minimum distance 14,
whereas shortening only controls a lower bound. The fol-
lowing lemma bridges this gap.
Lemma 6.2 (Distance normalization). For k ≥1 and
d ≥1, existence of a binary linear [n, k, ≥d] code implies
existence of a binary linear [n, k, d] code.
Proof. Let the original minimum distance be D ≥d, and
choose a word of weight D. Puncture D−d positions in its
support. Since D −d < D, puncturing is injective. Every
nonzero word retains weight at least D −(D −d) = d, and
the chosen word attains d. Append D −d zero coordinates
to restore length n without changing dimension or distance.
The hypothesis d ≥1 is essential: puncturing all D posi-
tions of a minimum-weight word would put that word in
the kernel. Zero padding is permitted because our domain
is the full class of binary linear codes.
Theorem 6.3. There is no binary linear [42, 16, ≥13]
code.
Proof. Parity extension of a hypothetical code gives
[43, 16, ≥14].
Shortening one coordinate loses at most
one dimension. Choose a 15-dimensional subcode, shorten
another coordinate, and choose a 14-dimensional subcode.
The result has parameters [41, 14, ≥14]. Lemma 6.2 gives
exact [41, 14, 14], contradicting E3, namely Proposition 2
of Bouyuklieva & Bouyukliev (2020).
The construction in this proof permits zero columns, which
is consistent with both our original domain and the binary
linear code domain of E3. The proof does not assume that
a minimum-weight word survives arbitrary shortening; nor-
malization is applied only after the shortening steps. We
regard Theorem 6.3 as a consequence of E3 and claim no
priority for it.
Corollary 6.4. The optimal distances satisfy
d2(43, 10) = 16,
d2(44, 10) = 17,
d2(44, 11) = 16,
d2(42, 16) = 12,
and 16 ≤d2(45, 11) ≤17.
Proof. Theorems 1.1 and 6.3 and Corollary 6.1 give the up-
per bounds. Appendix D speciﬁes full-rank generators of
[43, 10, 16], [44, 10, 17], [44, 11, 16], and [42, 16, 12] codes,
together with their fully enumerated weight spectra. Ap-
pending a zero coordinate to the [44, 11, 16] code gives a
[45, 11, 16] code.
The remaining possibility at parameters (45, 11) is a code
of minimum distance 17; the arguments above leave its ex-
istence undecided.
7. Concluding Remarks
We have shown that no binary linear [43, 10, ≥17] code
exists, and derived exact values of d2(n, k) at (43, 10),
(44, 10), (44, 11), and (42, 16). The MacWilliams identi-
ties force a dual word of weight four, which places a full-
support [40, 7, 18] subcode inside every hypothetical code;
a complete enumeration then shows that no two distinct ad-
missible even cosets of any of the 172 classiﬁed subcodes
are compatible. This obstruction is stronger than needed:
the triple-sum condition in (5) is never used. The published
classiﬁcations, catalogue inequivalence, and the enumera-
tion remain explicit inputs, both in the Lean development
and in the computation.
The case (45, 11) remains open: Corollary 6.1 excludes dis-
tance 18, but our arguments say nothing about a [45, 11, 17]
code. Parity extension of such a code would give an even
[46, 11, ≥18] code; we have not studied whether a reduc-
tion of the type used here applies to it.

A. Reproduction
A.1. Portable reproduction materials
The reproduction package is public at https://github.com/lisresearchgroup/math-rsi/tree/main/
plugins/ecc/archive/source/paper-reproduction. It supplies the literal catalogue matrices; 172 system-
atic input ﬁles and coordinate permutations; all 429,024 accepted representatives; both original C++ scanners; the original
pair-graph checker and witness-replay checker; and both pinned Lean source projects. The native source ﬁles are copied
without modiﬁcation. A dependency-free Python check validates matrices, input bindings, the polynomial (3), and recorded
cardinalities. A separate pynauty checker veriﬁes coordinate inequivalence.
The entry point scripts/reproduce_case.py builds the two scanners, re-enumerates both complete 232 domains
for a selected catalogue code, compares their outputs with each other and with the archived list, and recomputes every
pair distance. It accepts only complete runs and checks for a pair graph without edges. By default it prints the plan;
execution requires -run. Repeating it for indices 0 through 171 reproduces Proposition 5.1 from the supplied ﬁnite inputs.
It requires Python 3.10 or later and a C++17 compiler.
The approximately 1.68 GB of historical pair-witness streams are not part of the package. They are not needed to regenerate
the pair graphs with the supplied checker. Replaying those streams is a distinct and cheaper veriﬁcation path; it cannot
establish completeness of the supplied accepted lists.
A.2. Veriﬁcation record
On 2026-11-03, both original Lean projects were rebuilt and their audits passed. All lower-bound matrices and catalogue
parameters were rechecked, the 172 colored graphs were distinguished, both saved accepted sets were compared with
their coordinate bindings, and all 619,053,789 pair witnesses were replayed successfully. A further check of the portable
entry point fully regenerated cases 0 and 171 with both scanners and recomputed their pair graphs. The resulting list sizes
were 668 and 3,785, and both pair graphs were empty. These two end-to-end runs validate the packaging; the remaining
complete-domain scans retain their original run records.
